Anti-Dumping Duty Savings: The Hidden Cost Lever in Corrugated Plastic Sourcing

Corrugatedplasticusa Editorial • November 2026

Why Anti-Dumping Duties Belong in Your Cost Model

When procurement teams build a landed-cost model for corrugated plastic, they usually account for unit price, ocean freight, drayage, and duty at the standard harmonized tariff rate. What often gets missed is anti-dumping duty, or ADD, which is a separate assessment the US applies to specific products from specific countries found to be sold below fair market value. For polypropylene and corrugated plastic products, ADD can swing landed cost by double digits, and it is invisible until it lands on your customs entry.

The important thing to understand is that ADD is tied to country of origin, not to the product itself. The same twin-wall PP sheet manufactured in one country may carry a punitive anti-dumping rate while an identical sheet from another country carries none. For a procurement team buying tens of thousands of units a year, that difference is not a rounding error. It is a line item that can determine whether a program is profitable.

A Simplified Landed-Cost Comparison

Consider a buyer sourcing 20,000 corrugated plastic boxes at a base manufacturing cost of $4.00 per unit. Freight, drayage, and standard duty add roughly $1.20 per unit regardless of origin. The variable is ADD.

  • Origin with ADD exposure: If an anti-dumping order adds, say, 15% to the dutiable value, that is roughly $0.60 per unit in additional duty, pushing landed cost to about $5.80.
  • Origin at 0% ADD: With no anti-dumping assessment, landed cost holds near $5.20 per unit.
  • Annual impact: On 20,000 units, that $0.60 gap is $12,000 per year on a single SKU. Scaled across a multi-SKU program, the savings compound quickly.

These figures are illustrative, but the mechanism is real: country of origin is a cost lever you control at the sourcing decision, long before the first container ships.

Beyond Duty: Total Cost of Ownership

Duty savings are the headline, but a complete cost analysis also weighs durability and reuse. Corrugated plastic is a reusable packaging format, so cost-per-trip matters as much as cost-per-unit. A box that survives 50 round trips amortizes its purchase price across every cycle, and the ADD savings ride along on top of that amortization. When you combine a low landed cost with a high reuse count, the cost-per-trip advantage over single-use corrugated cardboard becomes decisive.

Buyers assembling a multi-category packaging program can keep that advantage consistent by sourcing across the Elipacko USA product line, which covers boxes, sheets, pallets, dividers, and containers from the same ADD-favorable origins and the same direct-manufacturer relationship.

How to Capture the Savings

To make sure the ADD advantage actually reaches your bottom line, take three steps. First, confirm the country of manufacture in writing on your purchase documents, since that is what customs uses to assess ADD. Second, request the supplier's compliance and resin documentation so your customs broker can classify the entry correctly. Third, model landed cost per trip rather than per unit, so the duty savings and the reuse advantage are both captured in the same number.
