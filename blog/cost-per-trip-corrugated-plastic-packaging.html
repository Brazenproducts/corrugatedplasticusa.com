Cost-Per-Trip Analysis: Corrugated Plastic Packaging for US Operations

Corrugatedplasticusa Editorial • October 2026

When procurement teams evaluate packaging, unit price is the wrong metric. The number that actually drives budget is cost-per-trip: the total landed cost of a container divided by the number of shipping cycles it survives. For US operations moving to reusable packaging, cost-per-trip exposes savings that a purchase-order line item never reveals.

Why Unit Price Misleads Procurement

A disposable corrugated box may cost under a dollar, but it completes exactly one trip. Factor in repeated purchasing, recurring freight on new stock, dunnage, and disposal fees, and the per-trip economics erode quickly. Reusable twin-wall polypropylene boxes carry a higher sticker price, yet they are engineered for dozens to hundreds of cycles. Spread that acquisition cost across the service life and the per-trip figure collapses well below disposable spend.

Building the Model

A defensible cost-per-trip model includes six inputs: acquisition cost, expected cycle life, reverse-logistics cost per return, cleaning or reconditioning cost, storage and float inventory, and shrinkage from loss or damage. For disposables, the model is simpler but relentless: unit price plus disposal, multiplied by every single shipment. The crossover point, where reusable cumulative spend dips below disposable, often arrives within the first operating year for closed-loop or semi-closed lanes.

Consider a lane shipping 2,000 units weekly. A disposable program at $0.85 per box and $0.12 disposal runs roughly $100,000 annually with zero residual value. A reusable fleet amortized over 50 trips, even with modest return and cleaning costs, routinely lands at a fraction of that once the fleet is sized correctly.

Sourcing Inputs That Favor the Model

The reusable case strengthens when acquisition cost is controlled. That duty position protects the per-trip math from tariff volatility that can quietly inflate landed cost on other imports.

Custom sizing also matters. Boxes dimensioned to the exact product and pallet footprint reduce void fill, improve cube utilization, and cut freight per unit, each of which feeds back into a lower cost-per-trip. The full Elipacko USA product line spans boxes, sheets, pallets, and containers, letting buyers standardize a reusable system rather than stitching together mismatched suppliers.

Accounting for Reverse Logistics

The most common objection to reusables is the return trip. Honest modeling accounts for it, but twin-wall PP boxes are often designed to collapse or nest, dramatically lowering return freight volume. A box that folds to a fraction of its erected height moves more empties per truck, which is where many programs recover the reverse-logistics cost that skeptics fear.

Shrinkage and Service Life

Loss is real, and the model should assume some attrition. The counterweight is durability: FDA-compliant resin formulations resist moisture, impact, and temperature swings that destroy fiber boxes, so the realized cycle count usually exceeds conservative planning assumptions. When a box survives 60 trips instead of a budgeted 50, the per-trip cost drops another 17% with no additional spend.

Making the Decision

Cost-per-trip turns a packaging purchase into an investment analysis. Build the model with real lane data, stress-test the return and shrinkage assumptions, and compare cumulative spend over a 24-month horizon rather than a single PO. For most US distribution operations running repeatable lanes, the reusable corrugated plastic case is not close, it is decisive. The discipline is simply refusing to let unit price stand in for total cost.
